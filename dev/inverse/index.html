Inverse Field Reconstruction ​

The inverse problem starts from measurements and reconstructs a field consistent with those data and explicit physical assumptions. It is not simply a forward solver run backwards: sparse data generally do not determine a unique field or current distribution.

Choosing an inverse method ​

The package currently provides three physically distinct models:

MethodRequired measurementsPhysical assumptionGuide and example
reconstruct_axisymmetric_fieldSpatial probes at (R, Z) with cylindrical (B_R, B_phi, B_Z) componentsAzimuthal symmetry about the z axis; pressure balance is not enforcedAxisymmetric guide, FLARE
reconstruct_field_3dCartesian vector and/or directional field probesNo spatial symmetry; a divergence-free representation, not pressure balance3D guide, tilted coil
reconstruct_grad_shafranovMagnetic-field and isotropic thermal-pressure time series, supplied invariant axis and crossing velocityPlanar translational invariance and magnetohydrostatic balanceSpacecraft GSR guide, mock crossing

An axisymmetric fit is not a cheaper general 3D model: applying it to a tilted coil or non-axisymmetric boundary discards real structure. Spacecraft GSR is not cylindrical Grad-Shafranov equilibrium reconstruction; it uses the planar Laplacian and requires pressure data and a supplied crossing frame.

Both spatial-probe methods accept exact boundary samples or uncertainty-weighted boundary observations. Exactness applies at supplied sample locations, not to the entire boundary. Neither infers a wall condition from the reconstruction box.

Shared workflow ​

  1. Select the physics first. Establish symmetry, equilibrium validity, domain, and the measurements actually available before choosing a solver.

  2. Prepare consistent data. Use metres, tesla, and the coordinate/component conventions of the selected method. Supply calibrated uncertainties where supported; default unit weights are not measurement-noise estimates.

  3. State constraints and priors. Distinguish measured boundary/current data from assumed constraints, known backgrounds, and regularization.

  4. Fit and evaluate. All three methods return callable fields evaluated at Cartesian positions in metres, returning Cartesian magnetic components in tesla. All support current_density; additional quantities are method-specific.

  5. Validate independently. Check held-out data, physical residuals, and sensitivity to basis resolution, regularization, and domain extent.

Validation and interpretation ​

  • Small training residuals do not establish uniqueness or off-probe accuracy.

  • Check current density as well as magnetic field: differentiation can amplify poorly constrained structure.

  • Compare with held-out probes and boundary locations that were not used to fit.

  • Repeat fits with plausible priors and numerical resolutions. For GSR, also vary continuation width, spatial degree, and step size.

  • Inspect available diagnostics and warnings. The 3D and GSR models expose field.diagnostics; these residuals are not posterior uncertainty estimates.

  • Keep synthetic truth out of the fit unless it represents genuinely known information, such as an explicitly supplied background field.

See reconstruction performance for 3D accuracy/cost measurements, and physical reconstruction cases for validation across sources and boundaries. The uncertainty and hidden-topology benchmark checks conditional noise propagation and demonstrates why it cannot establish topological uniqueness outside the fitted model.

Reconstructing topology from sparse measurements ​

The scientific target is often not just the field at unmeasured points, but its topology: magnetic nulls, separatrices, flux surfaces, and connections between regions or boundaries. A smooth divergence-free fit is a starting point, not evidence that these structures have been recovered.

In an axisymmetric field, critical points of poloidal flux identify poloidal O-points and X-points. With a nonzero toroidal field, these are not full magnetic nulls. In general 3D fields, connectivity requires field-line analysis; a contour plot or small vector-field residual is not a substitute. Near nulls and separatrices, small field errors can change inferred connections substantially.

What the present methods establish ​

The existing methods cover flexible divergence-free spatial fitting, source-informed axisymmetric fitting, and planar equilibrium continuation. They can generate candidate topologies under their respective assumptions. They do not currently quantify how uniquely sparse measurements determine those topologies.

Even complete normal-field data on a closed boundary do not determine an arbitrary current-carrying interior field. A divergence-free perturbation localized away from that boundary and the probes can preserve those observations while changing the interior field. Finite current samples reduce this freedom but do not remove it everywhere. Regularization chooses among admissible fields; it does not demonstrate that their topologies agree.

The useful question is therefore: which additional, independently justified information rules out topologically different fields that fit the data?

Which additional inverse approaches would help? ​

The following are candidate capabilities, not currently available solver options. Some are extensions to existing fits rather than separate reconstruction methods.

Force-free reconstructionCurrent is approximately parallel to the field and pressure forces are negligibleEnforce curl(B) = alpha B together with zero divergence; this couples field and current in a way the current spatial fits do notSparse probes and normal boundary data alone generally do not determine alpha; low pressure alone does not establish force-free balance

For a current-free, simply connected region, write B = -grad(Phi) with laplacian(Phi) = 0. Compatible normal-field data over the entire closed boundary determine the field, with the potential fixed only up to a constant. Sparse boundary samples do not provide that uniqueness. In multiply connected regions, a single-valued scalar potential may omit fields with nonzero circulation; the relevant global modes or linked-current information must also be supplied.

A conducting boundary is likewise not automatically a zero-normal-field boundary. Its magnetic condition depends on the assumed flux history and material model. Specify what is physically known, including external or far-field conditions, rather than treating the numerical box as a measured wall.

Force-free and magnetohydrostatic approaches should be conditional additions, not default upgrades. An equilibrium constraint is useful only where its neglected forces and time dependence are demonstrably small. The force-free review by Wiegelmann and Sakurai discusses the assumptions and boundary-data issues of that class of models.

How to decide whether another method is needed ​

Before adding a solver, compare the existing fits on synthetic problems designed to challenge topology, not only pointwise accuracy:

  1. Choose target observables: poloidal X/O-point locations, 3D null locations and types, separatrix geometry, or boundary-to-boundary connection classes. Define the domain and tracing termination rules for connectivity comparisons.

  2. Use known forward fields with and without the target structures. Include cases where probes miss a localized current region or a separatrix, and cases that deliberately violate an assumed symmetry or equilibrium.

  3. Hold out field, current, and boundary samples. Vary noise, probe placement, basis resolution, regularization, and plausible boundary assumptions.

  4. Search for alternative fits that agree with the measurements within their errors but have different target structures. Parameter sweeps or bootstrap fits are useful sensitivity checks, not by themselves calibrated posterior probabilities.

  5. Repeat with an independently justified source, vacuum, or equilibrium constraint. Check whether it improves topology recovery without hiding model mismatch or degrading held-out agreement.

The existing reconstruction cases provide forward truths and held-out validation. The uncertainty benchmark additionally checks null stability and an explicit topological ambiguity, but these controlled tests do not establish identifiability for a general experiment. A topology can be considered supported only to the extent that it persists across the admissible models, measurement errors, and numerical resolutions examined. If incompatible topologies remain, report the ambiguity rather than selecting one from its fit residual alone.

Recommended priorities ​

For sparse-measurement topology inference, the first priority is topology diagnostics and uncertainty/sensitivity analysis around the existing methods. This establishes whether the missing ingredient is information, representation, or physics; another flexible interpolator does not resolve a data null space.

The next method should follow the experimental regime:

  • Known coils and uncertain source strengths: extend source-informed inference, including general 3D configurations where axisymmetry is inappropriate.

  • Verified current-free measurement volume: prioritize a harmonic, geometry-aware reconstruction with explicit global circulation constraints where needed.

  • Current-carrying plasma: retain the divergence-free fit as a baseline, then assess force-free or pressure-balanced reconstruction only with supporting physical measurements.

Thus additional methods are warranted where they encode defensible information that the existing representations do not enforce. No method can guarantee a unique topology from arbitrary sparse probes and boundary samples alone.
